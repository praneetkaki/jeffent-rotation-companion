## **1\. Otic Drop Selection — The "Open Middle Ear" Rule**

**The single most important principle:** When the tympanic membrane is perforated OR a tympanostomy tube is present, the middle ear is *open* to the round/oval window — so the drop you choose can reach the inner ear. Choose a **non-ototoxic fluoroquinolone**, and **avoid aminoglycosides, alcohol, and low-pH/acidifying agents** because of ototoxicity and pain (Rosenfeld et al., AAO-HNSF Acute Otitis Externa CPG, 2014).

**Preferred agents when the middle ear is open (TM perforation or tubes):**

* **Ofloxacin otic** — FDA-approved for use with perforation/tubes; single agent.  
* **Ciprofloxacin \+ dexamethasone (Ciprodex)** — quinolone \+ steroid; approved for use with tubes and for acute otitis media with tubes.  
* Quinolones are **non-ototoxic** and are the recommended ototopical class in the US; for tube otorrhea and chronic suppurative otitis media, topical antibiotics outperform oral antibiotics because they deliver far higher local drug concentrations (Bhutta et al., Lancet, 2024; Venekamp et al., Cochrane, 2016).

**Agents to AVOID when the middle ear is open:**

* **Aminoglycoside drops** (neomycin, gentamicin, tobramycin) — potential cochleo- and vestibulotoxicity when the round window is exposed. Aminoglycosides enter cochlear hair cells, disrupt mitochondrial function, and cause **irreversible** hearing/balance loss (Illamola et al., Front Pharmacol, 2026).  
* **Neomycin/polymyxin B/hydrocortisone (Cortisporin)** — contains an aminoglycoside \+ low-pH vehicle; reserve for intact-TM otitis externa only.  
* Also avoid alcohol- and acid-based (antiseptic/acidifying) drops with a non-intact TM.

**If an aminoglycoside drop must be used** (e.g., discharging ear where no alternative is available): use **only with obvious active infection, for no longer than 2 weeks**, document the justification to the patient, and obtain baseline audiometry if practical (Phillips et al., ENT-UK consensus, Clin Otolaryngol, 2007).

**Intact TM (uncomplicated acute otitis externa):** the middle ear is closed, so ototoxicity is not the concern. Options include acidifying/antiseptic drops, aminoglycoside-containing combinations, or fluoroquinolone ± steroid drops; add a wick if the canal is edematous.

**Practical pearls:**

* A patent tube should be **assumed present** for \~12 months after placement (sometimes ≥3 years) unless extrusion and TM closure are documented — treat as an open middle ear (Rosenfeld et al., AAO-HNSF, 2014).  
* Patients who **taste** the drops or can blow air out the ear have a non-intact TM.  
* **Tragal pumping** and **aural toileting** before drops improve middle-ear penetration.  
* Adding a **topical steroid** reduces canal inflammation, but trial evidence that it improves discharge resolution over antibiotic alone is limited (Bhutta et al., Lancet, 2024).

## **2\. Steroid Regimens for Idiopathic Sudden Sensorineural Hearing Loss (ISSNHL)**

**Definition/urgency:** ≥30 dB SNHL across 3 contiguous frequencies over ≤72 hours. This is an **otologic emergency** — corticosteroids offer the greatest benefit within the **first 2 weeks**, with little benefit after 4–6 weeks; treat within 7 days of onset when possible (Chandrasekhar et al., AAO-HNSF Sudden Hearing Loss CPG, 2019). Always obtain audiometry to confirm SNHL and MRI to exclude retrocochlear pathology.

**Oral corticosteroid (standard first-line):**

* **Prednisone 1 mg/kg/day as a single (not divided) morning dose, usual maximum 60 mg/day.**  
* **Duration \~10–14 days**, including taper. Representative AAO-HNSF regimens:  
  1. Full dose × 4 days, then taper 10 mg every 2 days.  
  2. Full dose × 7–10 days, then taper over the following week (most common).  
  3. Full dose × 4 weeks then taper.  
* **Steroid-equivalent doses** (avoid underdosing): prednisone 60 mg ≈ methylprednisolone 48 mg ≈ dexamethasone 10 mg (Chandrasekhar et al., AAO-HNSF, 2019).  
* Counsel on hyperglycemia, insomnia, mood change, GI upset; caution in diabetes, uncontrolled HTN, PUD.

**Intratympanic (IT) corticosteroid:**

* **Dexamethasone (commonly 3.3–3.8 mg/mL) or methylprednisolone**, typically **3 injections spaced \~3–7 days apart** (Smith et al., STARFISH protocol, PLoS One, 2023).  
* **Roles:** (1) **salvage** after failed oral steroids; (2) **primary/first-line alternative** when systemic steroids are contraindicated (e.g., poorly controlled diabetes); (3) **combined** with oral steroids for severe/profound loss.  
* IT delivery achieves high inner-ear drug concentration while sparing systemic exposure; meta-analysis and a landmark RCT found IT steroids **non-inferior** to oral steroids as first-line therapy (Rauch et al., JAMA, 2011; Mirian & Ovesen, JAMA Otolaryngol Head Neck Surg, 2020).

**High-yield framing:** Oral is the default first-line; IT is the go-to for salvage and when systemic steroids are risky; combination is reasonable in severe loss. All routes are in widespread use and comparative efficacy is still being defined (STARFISH trial ongoing).

## **3\. Antibiotics for Peritonsillar Abscess (PTA) & Deep Neck Space Infections (DNSI)**

**Core principles:** infections are **polymicrobial** (aerobic gram-positives \+ oral anaerobes), so empiric therapy must cover *Streptococcus pyogenes*, *Streptococcus anginosus* group, *Staphylococcus aureus*, and anaerobes (*Fusobacterium necrophorum*, *Prevotella*). **Source control (drainage) plus IV antibiotics** is the paradigm; airway assessment comes first in DNSI (Darawish et al., Am J Otolaryngol, 2026).

**First-line empiric regimen:**

* **Ampicillin-sulbactam (Unasyn)** — the guideline-endorsed empiric first-line for PTA and DNSI. The β-lactamase inhibitor covers β-lactamase–producing anaerobes; near-complete susceptibility of PTA isolates including *F. necrophorum* (van de Loo et al., Sci Rep, 2026; Rudhart et al., PLoS One, 2025).  
* Alternative broad-spectrum β-lactam/β-lactamase combinations (e.g., amoxicillin-clavulanate for step-down oral therapy).

**Add vancomycin when MRSA is a concern:**

* **Add vancomycin** empirically if MRSA risk factors are present (prior MRSA, severe/toxic presentation, immunocompromise, failure to improve, high local MRSA prevalence) — MRSA is increasingly reported in DNSI (Darawish et al., Am J Otolaryngol, 2026).

**Role of metronidazole (Flagyl):**

* Provides **dedicated anaerobic coverage**. Not needed when using ampicillin-sulbactam (already covers anaerobes), but **add to a regimen lacking anaerobic activity** (e.g., a cephalosporin such as cefuroxime or ceftriaxone) — the cefuroxime \+ metronidazole pairing is a recognized alternative (Rudhart et al., PLoS One, 2025).

**Penicillin allergy:**

* **Clindamycin** is the standard alternative — covers streptococci, *S. aureus*, and anaerobes; PTA *F. necrophorum* isolates are essentially 100% clindamycin-susceptible (van de Loo et al., Sci Rep, 2026; Rudhart et al., PLoS One, 2025).

**Practical points:**

* **Drainage** (needle aspiration or I\&D for PTA; surgical drainage for DNSI abscess, typically \>2–2.5 cm, airway compromise, or medical failure) is essential — antibiotics alone are often insufficient.  
* Typical total antibiotic course **\~7–14 days** (mean \~8.5 days), with initial IV therapy \~5 days then oral step-down guided by clinical response (van de Loo et al., Sci Rep, 2026).  
* Watch for **Lemierre syndrome** (*F. necrophorum* septic internal jugular thrombophlebitis) and mediastinal/airway extension in DNSI.

**Quick reference — empiric antibiotic table:**

| Scenario | First-line | Penicillin allergy | Add-on |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| PTA / DNSI, standard | Ampicillin-sulbactam IV | Clindamycin | Vancomycin if MRSA risk |
| Cephalosporin-based regimen | Ceftriaxone or cefuroxime | — | **Metronidazole** for anaerobes |
| Severe/toxic or MRSA risk | Ampicillin-sulbactam **\+ vancomycin** | Clindamycin ± vancomycin | — |

## **4\. Antimicrobial Classes — High-Yield Reference for Head & Neck Infections**

**Penicillins (bactericidal; inhibit cell-wall synthesis):**

* **Natural penicillins (penicillin G/V):** gram-positive cocci, gram-negative cocci, some gram-negative rods, plus spirochetes and actinomyces; susceptible to all β-lactamases.  
* **Antistaphylococcal penicillins (oxacillin, nafcillin, dicloxacillin):** methicillin-sensitive staphylococci, penicillin-susceptible streptococci, anaerobic GPC; **not active against gram-negatives**; β-lactamase resistant.  
* **Aminopenicillins (ampicillin, amoxicillin):** essentially the same spectrum as natural penicillins plus *Haemophilus influenzae*; susceptible to β-lactamases.  
* **Carboxypenicillins (ticarcillin-clavulanate):** increased gram-negative coverage including *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*.  
* **Acylureidopenicillins (piperacillin-tazobactam):** excellent gram-positive and gram-negative coverage including *P. aeruginosa*; enhanced activity against some β-lactamases.

**Cephalosporins (bactericidal; interfere with cell-wall synthesis) — by generation:**

* **3rd generation** (IV ceftriaxone, ceftazidime; oral cefixime, cefditoren): increased gram-negative activity; ceftriaxone also covers penicillin-resistant *S. pneumoniae*; ceftazidime covers *Pseudomonas*.  
* **4th generation** (cefepime — only US agent): broadest gram-negative activity, including *P. aeruginosa*.  
* **5th generation / MRSA-active** (ceftaroline — only approved agent): excellent activity against MRSA and other gram-positives including *S. pneumoniae*; gram-negative activity similar to ceftriaxone; currently FDA-approved for pneumonia and skin/soft-tissue infections.

**Penicillin allergy and cephalosporin cross-reactivity:**

* **Penicillin skin testing is recommended when available.** A negative skin test means **no increased cross-reactivity** to cephalosporins.  
* Only \~10% of patients reporting penicillin allergy have positive skin tests, and only \~3.4% of skin-test-positive patients react to cephalosporins — **low but non-zero risk**.  
* Consider the **nature of the reaction**: if severe (anaphylaxis), either avoid cephalosporins or give via **graded challenge** or **rapid desensitization**.

**Carbapenems (bactericidal; inhibit cell-wall synthesis):**

* Four US agents — **ertapenem, imipenem, doripenem, meropenem** — all with very broad activity (gram-positives, resistant gram-negatives, anaerobes).  
* **Ertapenem is the least broad**: it lacks activity against *Enterococcus* sp. and *P. aeruginosa*.

**Fluoroquinolones (bactericidal; inhibit DNA gyrase):**

* Common coverage: gram-negative bacilli (Enterobacteriaceae, *H. influenzae*), *Neisseria*, *Moraxella catarrhalis*, and atypicals (*Legionella*, *Mycoplasma*, *Chlamydophila*).  
* **Ciprofloxacin:** broadest gram-negative activity including *P. aeruginosa*; **limited streptococcal activity** (not typically used for head & neck infections).  
* **Levofloxacin, moxifloxacin, gatifloxacin:** best streptococcal ("respiratory") activity; levofloxacin also covers *P. aeruginosa*.

**Macrolides (bacteriostatic; inhibit protein synthesis):**

* Three agents — **erythromycin, azithromycin, clarithromycin**. Broad gram-positive and gram-negative activity; azithromycin/clarithromycin have broader spectrum, better absorption, and fewer GI effects than erythromycin.

**Clindamycin (bacteriostatic; inhibit protein synthesis):**

* Good gram-positive coverage including **MRSA** and streptococci (*S. pyogenes*, *S. pneumoniae*, viridans group).  
* Signature feature is **anaerobic coverage** — the adage is "anaerobes above the diaphragm" (oral-cavity *Peptostreptococcus*, *Veillonella*), making it an **ideal choice for odontogenic infection**.

**TMP-SMX (bacteriostatic; folate antagonist):**

* Broad aerobic gram-positive and gram-negative activity — MSSA and **MRSA**, *S. pneumoniae*, *M. catarrhalis*, *H. influenzae*, enteric gram-negative bacilli.  
* Key toxicities: GI upset, rash, **Stevens-Johnson syndrome / TEN**, and nephrotoxicity.

**Tetracyclines (bacteriostatic; inhibit protein synthesis):**

* Doxycycline, minocycline, tetracycline — broad gram-positive/gram-negative activity including *S. pneumoniae*, *H. influenzae*, *Mycoplasma*; tigecycline (IV) adds resistant gram-negatives.  
* **Contraindicated in children and pregnant women** (effects on developing teeth and bones).

**Anti-MRSA agents:**

* **IV:** vancomycin (inhibits cell-wall synthesis), daptomycin, linezolid, ceftaroline.  
* **Oral:** TMP-SMX, clindamycin, tetracyclines, linezolid.  
* Fluoroquinolones have anti-staphylococcal (including MRSA) activity but **should not be used as monotherapy** for staphylococcal infection — resistance develops rapidly.

**Antifungals (relevant to invasive head & neck fungal disease):**

* **Azoles** (fluconazole, itraconazole, posaconazole, voriconazole): fluconazole for *Candida*; **voriconazole is first-line for aspergillosis**.  
* **Echinocandins** (micafungin, caspofungin, anidulafungin): invasive candidiasis including *C. krusei* and *C. glabrata*.  
* **Amphotericin B** (polyene): reserved for serious infection including **invasive rhinosinusitis due to mucormycosis**; the **liposomal form is preferred** over conventional due to less nephrotoxicity.

***Clostridioides difficile*** **colitis — antibiotic risk stratification:**

* Presents with diffuse watery diarrhea, often with **leukocytosis** (may precede diarrhea); fever, bloody stool, and abdominal pain suggest severe disease. Prior antibiotic use is the primary risk factor.  
* **Highest-risk antibiotics:** clindamycin, fluoroquinolones, cephalosporins, carbapenems.  
* **Lower-risk:** macrolides, penicillins, sulfonamides.
