## **1\. Otology / Neurotology**

### **Case Brief — Myringotomy & Tympanostomy Tubes**

**Vignette:** A 2-year-old is referred after four episodes of acute otitis media in the past 12 months, the most recent three weeks ago. Bilateral effusions have persisted since. Parents report she is not turning to soft sounds and speech is delayed.

**Decision points:**

* Does she meet criteria for tubes? Yes — recurrent AOM (≥3 in 6 months or ≥4 in 12 months with one recent) OR bilateral OME ≥3 months with hearing/developmental concern.  
* Get an audiogram and tympanometry before surgery to document baseline hearing and effusion.  
* Where is the incision? Anteroinferior quadrant — away from ossicles, chorda tympani, and jugular bulb.

**Key operative steps:**

1. Position, place the ear speculum, and clear the canal of cerumen under the microscope.  
2. Bring the tympanic membrane into full view and orient to the malleus and light reflex.  
3. Make a radial myringotomy incision in the anteroinferior (or inferior) quadrant.  
4. Aspirate middle-ear effusion (serous, mucoid, or purulent).  
5. Insert the tympanostomy tube with a Rosen needle/alligator and seat both flanges.  
6. Instill ototopical antibiotic drops if indicated.

**Key danger structures:** Ossicular chain and chorda tympani (posterosuperior quadrant), a dehiscent/high jugular bulb (posteroinferior floor), and the round window niche.

**Pearl:** The most common postoperative issue is tube otorrhea — treat with ototopical fluoroquinolone drops, not systemic antibiotics.

### **Case Brief — Tympanoplasty / Mastoidectomy for Cholesteatoma**

**Vignette:** A 34-year-old presents with chronic foul-smelling otorrhea and progressive hearing loss in one ear. Otoscopy shows a retraction pocket with white keratin debris in the pars flaccida. CT shows a soft-tissue mass eroding the scutum.

**Decision points:**

* What is it? Attic (pars flaccida) cholesteatoma in Prussak's space.  
* Why urgent? It erodes bone — risking ossicular destruction, labyrinthine fistula, facial palsy, and intracranial spread.  
* CWU vs. CWD? Canal-wall-up preserves anatomy but has higher recurrence and needs a second-look; canal-wall-down lowers recurrence but leaves a mastoid bowl needing lifelong cleaning.

**Key operative steps:**

1. Postauricular incision; harvest temporalis fascia/tragal cartilage graft and elevate a tympanomeatal flap.  
2. Cortical mastoidectomy — drill within the boundaries of tegmen (superior), sigmoid sinus (posterior), bony canal (anterior), using the MacEwen triangle as the surface landmark for the antrum \[1\]\[3\].  
3. Identify the antrum, lateral semicircular canal, and short process of the incus as key landmarks.  
4. Perform a posterior tympanotomy (facial recess) if middle-ear access is needed.  
5. Remove disease completely, decide CWU vs. CWD based on extent/recurrence risk, and address the ossicular chain.  
6. Graft the tympanic membrane defect and reconstruct/ossiculoplasty as needed.

**Key danger structures:** Facial nerve (tympanic/mastoid segments, facial recess), lateral semicircular canal (fistula), sigmoid sinus, tegmen/dura, and the ossicular chain \[1\]\[3\].

**Pearl:** New vertigo with a positive fistula test signals a labyrinthine fistula — a red flag that changes surgical planning.

## **2\. Rhinology / Sinus & Skull Base**

### **Case Brief — Endoscopic Sinus Surgery**

**Vignette:** A 45-year-old with 6 months of nasal congestion, facial pressure, and anosmia has failed saline irrigation, intranasal steroids, and a course of antibiotics. CT shows bilateral ostiomeatal complex obstruction and ethmoid opacification.

**Decision points:**

* Indication met? Failure of appropriate medical therapy plus objective disease on CT/endoscopy.  
* Review the CT for danger zones: Keros classification (skull-base height), lamina papyracea integrity, anterior ethmoid artery position, and any dehiscence.  
* Most feared complications: CSF leak (cribriform/fovea ethmoidalis), orbital injury (medial rectus/optic nerve), carotid injury near the sphenoid.

**Key operative steps:**

1. Decongest, then medialize the middle turbinate and identify the uncinate process \[40\].  
2. Uncinectomy to expose the infundibulum \[39\]\[40\].  
3. Maxillary antrostomy — locate the natural ostium behind the uncinate and enlarge posteriorly/inferiorly, incorporating the natural ostium to avoid recirculation \[39\]\[44\].  
4. Anterior ethmoidectomy — open the ethmoid bulla and clear cells to the basal lamella \[39\]\[40\].  
5. Posterior ethmoidectomy through the basal lamella, identifying the skull base and lamina papyracea \[39\]\[40\].  
6. Sphenoidotomy — resect the inferior superior turbinate, find the sphenoid ostium medial to the superior turbinate, and widen \[39\].  
7. Frontal recess dissection last, as needed.

**Key danger structures:** Lamina papyracea/orbit (medial rectus, optic nerve), skull base at the cribriform/fovea ethmoidalis (CSF leak), anterior ethmoid artery, and the internal carotid artery in the lateral sphenoid wall \[39\]\[44\].

**Pearl:** Suspected intraoperative or postoperative clear rhinorrhea — send fluid for beta-2 transferrin (most specific for CSF).

### **Case Brief — Epistaxis**

**Vignette:** A 60-year-old on apixaban presents with 45 minutes of brisk right-sided nosebleed. Direct pressure at home failed. He is spitting up blood and no anterior source is seen after decongestion.

**Decision points:**

* Anterior vs. posterior? Failure of firm compression of the lower-third nose plus posterior bleeding/no visible anterior source suggests a posterior (sphenopalatine) bleed.  
* Escalation: topical vasoconstrictor → cautery if a source is seen → anterior/posterior packing → SPA ligation or IMAX embolization if refractory.  
* Address the coagulopathy and check hemodynamics; posterior packs need monitoring for airway compromise/hypoxia.

**Key operative steps (escalating):**

1. Firm compression of the lower-third (soft) nose \~15 minutes leaning forward, plus topical oxymetazoline.  
2. Anterior rhinoscopy/endoscopy after decongestion to localize the source.  
3. Chemical (silver nitrate) or electrocautery of a visualized anterior source (avoid bilateral septal cautery).  
4. Anterior packing if diffuse/no clear source; add posterior packing for posterior bleeds.  
5. Endoscopic sphenopalatine artery ligation for refractory posterior bleeding.  
6. Interventional radiology (internal maxillary artery embolization) if surgery fails or the patient is a poor operative candidate.

**Key danger structures:** Sphenopalatine artery (posterior source), anterior/posterior ethmoid arteries (skull base, can retract into orbit), and the septal cartilage (perforation risk with bilateral cautery).

**Pearl:** Unilateral recurrent epistaxis with obstruction in an adolescent male suggests juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma — image it, do NOT biopsy in clinic.

### **Case Brief — Transsphenoidal Hypophysectomy**

**Vignette:** A 52-year-old with bitemporal hemianopia and headaches has an MRI showing a 2.2 cm sellar mass with suprasellar extension compressing the optic chiasm. Labs confirm a nonfunctioning adenoma with partial hypopituitarism.

**Decision points:**

* Indication? Mass effect (visual loss, chiasmal compression) and a macroadenoma with extrasellar extension — surgical.  
* Remember: prolactinomas are treated medically first, not surgically.  
* Border structures at risk: carotids laterally, optic nerves/chiasm superiorly, cavernous sinus (CN III, IV, V1–V2, VI) laterally.

**Key operative steps:**

1. Endoscopic transnasal approach; lateralize/resect the middle turbinate as needed and preserve superior turbinate/cranial septal mucosa to spare olfaction \[6\].  
2. Unilateral posterior septectomy/sphenoethmoidectomy and (if needed) septoplasty to widen the corridor \[6\]\[9\].  
3. Wide anterior sphenoidotomy and removal of sphenoid septations to expose the sella \[6\]\[7\].  
4. Open the sellar floor and perform an X-shaped durotomy \[6\].  
5. Intradural adenoma resection using standard/angled techniques \[6\].  
6. Skull-base reconstruction (fat graft, nasoseptal flap) and sphenoid packing \[6\].

**Key danger structures:** Internal carotid arteries (lateral sphenoid wall), optic nerves/chiasm (superolateral), cavernous sinus contents (CN III, IV, V1–V2, VI), and the diaphragma sellae/arachnoid (CSF leak) \[7\].

**Pearl:** Watch the postoperative endocrine course — transient diabetes insipidus is the most common early problem, and delayed hyponatremia (SIADH) around days 5–9 is the leading cause of readmission. CSF leak is the most common surgical complication.

## **3\. Laryngology**

### **Case Brief — Flexible Laryngoscopy & Vocal Fold Disorders**

**Vignette:** A 30-year-old schoolteacher reports 3 months of hoarseness and vocal fatigue. Flexible laryngoscopy shows symmetric bilateral lesions at the junction of the anterior and middle thirds of the vocal folds.

**Decision points:**

* What are they? Vocal fold nodules — bilateral, at the point of maximal vibratory contact, from voice overuse.  
* First-line management is voice therapy, not surgery.  
* Contrast with a paramedian, immobile cord (RLN injury) or a unilateral polyp/cyst.

**Key operative steps (microflap excision / injection laryngoplasty):**

1. Suspension microlaryngoscopy under general anesthesia (or awake transoral/transcervical injection for medialization) \[11\]\[13\].  
2. Assess the lesion and vibratory margin with a 0° rigid endoscope and microscope \[11\].  
3. For a benign lesion: raise a subepithelial microflap, preserving the vocal ligament and superficial lamina propria.  
4. Excise the lesion (cold instruments preferred over laser for benign disease to protect the layered structure).  
5. For glottic insufficiency: inject a medialization material (temporary gelfoam/collagen or durable calcium hydroxylapatite) lateral to the vocalis until midline closure \[10\]\[13\].  
6. Confirm glottic closure and voice with intraoperative phonation when awake.

**Key danger structures:** The layered lamina propria/vocal ligament (over-resection causes scar), the anterior commissure (webbing), and the superior laryngeal neurovascular bundle in the supraglottic "danger triangle" during CO2 laser work \[14\].

**Pearl:** A breathy, hoarse voice with a cord fixed in paramedian position after neck/thyroid surgery points to recurrent laryngeal nerve injury; a normal-looking cord with pitch loss points to the external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve.

## **4\. Head & Neck Surgery**

### **Case Brief — Tonsillectomy & Adenoidectomy**

**Vignette:** A 6-year-old has loud snoring, witnessed apneas, and restless sleep. Exam shows 3+ obstructing tonsils. Separately, an older sibling had 7 culture-positive throat infections last year.

**Decision points:**

* Two classic indications: obstructive sleep-disordered breathing/OSA and recurrent infection by Paradise criteria (≥7 in 1 yr, ≥5/yr × 2 yr, ≥3/yr × 3 yr).  
* Screen for submucous cleft/bifid uvula before adenoidectomy — risk of velopharyngeal insufficiency.  
* Postoperative analgesia: acetaminophen ± ibuprofen; minimize opioids.

**Key operative steps:**

1. Oral RAE intubation; place the mouth gag (e.g., Crowe-Davis) and confirm airway/tongue not compressed.  
2. Retract the tonsil medially and incise the mucosa over the superior pole.  
3. Develop the plane between the tonsil capsule and the pharyngeal musculature (extracapsular) — or debride to the capsule for intracapsular technique \[24\]\[31\].  
4. Dissect superior-to-inferior and remove the tonsil at the inferior pole.  
5. Achieve hemostasis (electrocautery/ties) in the tonsillar fossa.  
6. Adenoidectomy by curettage, suction cautery, or microdebrider under mirror/endoscopic view \[24\]\[25\].

**Key danger structures:** The tonsillar branch of the facial artery and adjacent great vessels (the internal carotid lies just lateral to the fossa), the pharyngeal musculature, and — during adenoidectomy — the eustachian tube orifices and velopharynx \[29\].

**Pearl:** A post-tonsillectomy bleed can be catastrophic — ABCs, IV access, type & screen, and return to OR for brisk bleeding. Secondary bleeds classically occur around postoperative days 5–10.

### **Case Brief — Thyroidectomy**

**Vignette:** A 48-year-old woman has a 2.5 cm thyroid nodule. TSH is normal, ultrasound is TR4, and FNA returns Bethesda V (suspicious for malignancy). Total thyroidectomy is planned.

**Decision points:**

* Nerves at risk: recurrent laryngeal nerve (near the inferior thyroid artery and the ligament of Berry) and the external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve.  
* Identify the RLN in the tracheoesophageal groove within Beahrs' triangle; the tubercle of Zuckerkandl points to it.  
* Anticipate the most common complication: transient hypocalcemia — check calcium/PTH; watch for perioral numbness, Chvostek/Trousseau signs.

**Key operative steps:**

1. Curvilinear incision \~2 fingerbreadths above the sternal notch; divide platysma and separate the strap muscles in the midline \[18\].  
2. Rotate the lobe medially; identify, divide, and ligate the middle thyroid vein \[18\].  
3. Take the superior pole close to the capsule to protect the external branch of the SLN \[18\].  
4. Identify and preserve the superior and inferior parathyroid glands, keeping their blood supply \[17\]\[18\].  
5. Identify and trace the RLN in the tracheoesophageal groove (Beahrs'/Simon's triangle); ligate inferior thyroid artery branches on the capsule \[15\]\[18\].  
6. Reidentify the RLN at the ligament of Berry and dissect it off the trachea; repeat contralaterally for total thyroidectomy \[18\].

**Key danger structures:** Recurrent laryngeal nerve (esp. at Berry's ligament and near the inferior thyroid artery), external branch of the SLN (superior pole), the parathyroid glands and their vascular pedicles, and the trachea/esophagus \[15\]\[17\]\[18\].

**Pearl:** Postoperative stridor and airway obstruction on extubation suggest bilateral RLN injury — be ready to reintubate or perform tracheostomy.

### **Case Brief — Parotidectomy & Neck Dissection**

**Vignette:** A 55-year-old has a slow-growing, painless mass at the angle of the mandible. Exam shows a mobile mass with an intact facial nerve. FNA suggests pleomorphic adenoma.

**Decision points:**

* Most common benign parotid tumor is pleomorphic adenoma (Warthin is second, bilateral, smokers); mucoepidermoid is the most common malignancy.  
* Identify the facial nerve trunk: tragal pointer, tympanomastoid suture, posterior belly of digastric.  
* Counsel on Frey syndrome (gustatory sweating) as a delayed complication.

**Key operative steps:**

1. Modified Blair incision; raise the skin-SMAS flap over the parotid.  
2. Identify the great auricular nerve and external jugular vein; free the tail of the parotid from the SCM.  
3. Locate the facial nerve main trunk using the tragal pointer, tympanomastoid suture, and posterior belly of digastric.  
4. Dissect the nerve branches anteriorly, separating superficial from deep lobe along the facial nerve plane.  
5. Remove the tumor with a cuff of normal gland (superficial or total parotidectomy).  
6. Confirm nerve integrity (stimulator), obtain hemostasis, and close over a drain.

**Key danger structures:** Facial nerve (trunk and branches — especially marginal mandibular), the retromandibular vein and external carotid artery within the gland, the great auricular nerve, and the auriculotemporal nerve (Frey syndrome).

**Pearl:** A cystic neck mass in a middle-aged adult is metastatic HPV-associated oropharyngeal SCC until proven otherwise — never assume a branchial cleft cyst; do FNA and imaging.

## **5\. Facial Plastic & Reconstructive Surgery**

### **Case Brief — Facial Trauma & Nasal Fracture**

**Vignette:** A 22-year-old presents after a sports injury with a deformed, swollen nose and epistaxis. On exam there is a bluish, boggy swelling on the septum.

**Decision points:**

* Rule out first: septal hematoma (drain urgently) and CSF rhinorrhea (beta-2 transferrin).  
* Untreated septal hematoma → cartilage necrosis → saddle-nose deformity.  
* For associated orbital trauma, check for enophthalmos, diplopia on upgaze, and infraorbital hypesthesia (blowout fracture).

**Key operative steps:**

1. Inspect intranasally with a speculum for septal hematoma; if present, incise, drain, and pack/quilt to prevent reaccumulation \[45\]\[47\].  
2. Time the closed reduction — immediately before swelling sets in, or at \~3–5 days (children) / up to \~10–14 days once swelling subsides \[45\]\[46\].  
3. Anesthetize (topical/local or general) \[46\].  
4. Reduce the nasal bones with a Boies elevator intranasally plus external molding \[46\].  
5. Reduce/align the septum \[46\].  
6. Apply internal (packing/splints) and external nasal splints for support \[46\].

**Key danger structures:** The septal cartilage blood supply (necrosis/saddle-nose from untreated hematoma), the cribriform plate (CSF leak), and the medial canthal/lacrimal apparatus with naso-orbito-ethmoid involvement \[45\]\[47\].

**Pearl:** In a child with an orbital "trapdoor" fracture and bradycardia/nausea (oculocardiac reflex from muscle entrapment), repair is a surgical urgency, not a delayed procedure.

## **6\. Pediatric Otolaryngology**

### **Case Brief — Pediatric Airway**

**Vignette:** A 4-month-old has noisy breathing since birth. The inspiratory stridor worsens when supine, feeding, and crying, and improves when prone. Growth and feeding are otherwise adequate.

**Decision points:**

* Most likely: laryngomalacia — the most common cause of infant stridor, usually self-resolving.  
* Remember the narrowest part of the pediatric airway is the subglottis (cricoid ring).  
* Distinguish emergent mimics: croup (steeple sign), epiglottitis (thumbprint sign — do NOT agitate), foreign body (unilateral wheeze, air trapping).

**Key operative steps (direct laryngoscopy & bronchoscopy):**

1. Shared airway plan with anesthesia; maintain spontaneous ventilation.  
2. Perform flexible laryngoscopy awake to assess dynamic collapse (laryngomalacia).  
3. Direct laryngoscopy/rigid bronchoscopy to evaluate the supraglottis, glottis, subglottis, and trachea.  
4. Treat identified pathology (e.g., supraglottoplasty for severe laryngomalacia; retrieve a foreign body with optical forceps).  
5. Reassess for a synchronous second airway lesion.

**Key danger structures:** The subglottis (cricoid — post-procedure edema/stenosis), the true vocal folds, the teeth/lips, and the friable airway mucosa; for foreign bodies, the risk of distal migration and complete obstruction.

**Pearl:** An esophageal button battery is a true emergency — liquefactive necrosis within hours mandates emergent removal.

## **7\. Sleep Surgery**

### **Case Brief — OSA & Sleep Surgery**

**Vignette:** A 50-year-old man with a BMI of 30 has an AHI of 28 (moderate OSA) and cannot tolerate CPAP after several months of trials. He asks about surgical options.

**Decision points:**

* Grade severity by AHI: mild 5–15, moderate 15–30, severe \>30.  
* CPAP is first-line; surgery is for intolerance/failure or a specific anatomic obstruction.  
* DISE identifies the level(s) of collapse to tailor surgery.

**Key operative steps (UPPP, illustrative):**

1. Orotracheal intubation and mouth-gag exposure.  
2. Tonsillectomy (if tonsils present).  
3. Trim and reposition the posterior tonsillar pillar.  
4. Excise redundant soft palate/uvular mucosa conservatively (avoid over-resection).  
5. Suture the anterior and posterior pillars/palatal flaps to widen the retropalatal airway.  
6. Achieve hemostasis and confirm an adequate velopharyngeal port.

**Key danger structures:** The velopharynx (over-resection → velopharyngeal insufficiency and nasopharyngeal stenosis), the great vessels lateral to the tonsillar fossa, and the airway itself (postoperative edema).

**Pearl:** OSA patients are high-risk perioperatively — sensitivity to sedatives/opioids, difficult airway, and postoperative respiratory depression; minimize opioids and monitor closely.

### **Case Brief — Hypoglossal Nerve Stimulator (HGNS) Implantation**

**Vignette:** The same CPAP-intolerant patient has moderate-to-severe OSA, a BMI below the device threshold, and DISE shows anteroposterior tongue-base collapse without complete concentric palatal collapse.

**Decision points:**

* Candidacy: moderate-to-severe OSA, CPAP failure/intolerance, BMI below threshold (\~\<32–35), and absence of complete concentric palatal collapse (a contraindication).  
* Mechanism: inspiration-synchronized stimulation of the protrusor branches of CN XII advances the tongue via the genioglossus.  
* Branch selection is the crux: the medial division (m-XII) supplies protrusors (genioglossus, transverse/vertical intrinsics) and is included in the cuff; the lateral division (l-XII) supplies retractors (styloglossus, hyoglossus) and is excluded.

**Key operative steps (classic 3-incision technique):**

1. Place EMG electrodes into the genioglossus/lateral tongue and map the three incisions \[33\]\[38\].  
2. Submandibular incision \~1 fingerbreadth below the mandible (submandibular-gland-type incision) to reach the hypoglossal nerve \[33\]\[38\].  
3. Dissect and isolate CN XII; use neurostimulation to confirm the inclusion (protrusor/medial) branches and exclude retractor (lateral) fibers and the C1 twig, then place the stimulation cuff \[33\].  
4. Create an infraclavicular chest pocket for the pulse generator \[33\]\[38\].  
5. Place the respiratory sensing lead between the internal and external intercostal muscles \[33\]\[38\].  
6. Tunnel the leads (protecting the external jugular vein), connect to the generator, and test extensively before closure \[33\]\[38\].

**Key danger structures:** The lateral (retractor) branch of CN XII and the C1 nerve twig (must be excluded from the cuff), Wharton's duct and the ranine/lingual vein, the marginal mandibular nerve (submandibular incision), the external jugular vein (tunneling), and the pleura (sensing-lead placement) \[33\].

**Pearl:** Intraoperative EMG confirms correct capture — medial-branch stimulation produces genioglossus protrusion, lateral-branch stimulation produces retraction; the cuff sits superficial to the hyoglossus where inclusion and exclusion fibers can be separated \[33\].

## **8\. Cross-Cutting**

### **Case Brief — Tracheostomy**

**Vignette:** A ventilated ICU patient 4 days after tracheostomy suddenly develops respiratory distress and the tube appears partially dislodged during repositioning.

**Decision points:**

* Is the tract mature? No — maturation takes \~5–7 days, so do NOT blindly reinsert an immature tube.  
* Secure the airway from above: orally intubate/bag-mask ventilate as needed and call for help.  
* Beware creating a false passage into pretracheal soft tissue — confirm intraluminal placement with capnography or bronchoscopy.

**Key operative steps (open tracheostomy):**

1. Extend the neck; make a horizontal incision midway between the cricoid and sternal notch.  
2. Divide platysma and separate the strap muscles in the midline.  
3. Retract or divide the thyroid isthmus to expose the trachea.  
4. Identify the cricoid and tracheal rings; place a cricoid hook and stay sutures.  
5. Create the tracheal opening between the 2nd–3rd (or 3rd–4th) rings.  
6. Insert the tracheostomy tube, confirm placement with capnography, and secure with sutures/ties.

**Key danger structures:** The thyroid isthmus, the anterior jugular veins, the innominate (brachiocephalic) artery low in the neck (tracheo-innominate fistula), the esophagus/posterior tracheal wall, and the recurrent laryngeal nerves in the tracheoesophageal grooves.

**Pearl:** A "herald" sentinel bleed from a trach signals a possible tracheo-innominate fistula — hyperinflate the cuff or apply digital compression (Utley maneuver) and go emergently to the OR. The most common cause of acute distress overall is a mucus plug — prevented with humidification, suctioning, and inner-cannula care.
